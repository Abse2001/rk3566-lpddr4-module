rk3566-lpddr4-module · board v0.1.14 · simulated, not yet measured

Is every fast signal on the module clean?

The module carries three kinds of fast wiring: the RAM bus between the RK3566 and the LPDDR4X chip, 17 differential pairs (HDMI, MIPI camera/display, PCIe, USB), and the vias that move signals between the six copper layers. Each check below says what was tested, shows the result, and explains how to read the chart. All charts come from simulations of the real copper on the board.

1

RAM signals open a clean "eye"

An eye diagram overlays many bits of one signal on top of each other, two bit-times wide. Clean bits stack into an open shape that looks like an eye. Noise, reflections and slow edges close it. The receiver samples in the middle, so what matters is how tall (voltage margin) and how wide (timing margin) the opening stays.

How to read itEach thin line is one bit. The shaded box is the clear opening ngspice measured; the bigger it is relative to the whole bit, the more margin. A DQ line (data) is single-ended and swings 0 to 0.6 V on LPDDR4X; CK (clock) is a differential pair, so its swing is plus/minus.

What it means: at the speed RK3566 boards normally run the RAM (1056 MHz, 2112 MT/s) the data eye is open for about three quarters of a bit. At the chip's maximum (1560 MHz, 3120 MT/s) it is still open for about two thirds. The model uses a generic driver, not Rockchip's real buffer (no public IBIS model), so treat the exact margin as indicative.

2

RAM timing stays inside Rockchip's rules

Rockchip's layout guide limits how far each group of RAM wires may drift in arrival time: the two halves of the clock pair, the command lines against the clock, each data byte against its strobe. Each bar shows how much of the allowed window the board uses.

3

Differential pairs arrive together

A differential pair sends the signal on one wire (P) and its mirror image on the other (N). The receiver only looks at the difference, which is what makes these links fast and quiet. That only works if P and N switch at the same moment. The gap between them is the skew. Our rule is 0.76 ps (5 mil of copper), which is strict; the interfaces themselves tolerate more.

How to read itP (blue) rises while N (orange, drawn flipped so both rise) falls. Where the two lines cross the midpoint is when the receiver sees the bit change. If they cross at the same time the pair is matched; a horizontal gap between them is skew.

What it means: where P and N use the same number of vias, the pair is matched to well under 1 ps, including the two pairs re-routed with equal vias in v0.1.14 (MIPI CSI D2 went from 18.6 ps to 1.3 ps, PCIe TX from 4.9 ps to 0.3 ps). Where the counts differ, extra trace length can only approximate a via: re-tuning with the measured via delay cut the worst pair from 19.1 ps to 8.4 ps, but three pairs ended slightly worse (by 0.5 to 1.9 ps). The fix that works is routing those eight pairs with equal vias.

4

What a via really costs

A via is a plated hole that carries a signal between layers. It is short, but it is not free: the barrel adds delay, and its pad adds capacitance that slows the edge. The old length-matching rule counted every via as 0.6 mm of trace. Simulating a via with its pad capacitance showed it costs about twice that, which is why pairs with an extra via came out skewed.

5

Drill-to-copper spacing

JLCPCB asks for 0.2 mm between a drilled via hole and any copper of another signal, and its DFM tool marks anything under 0.18 mm as "Danger". The router keeps copper 0.1 mm from every via pad, which guarantees at least 0.15 mm to the hole. A push solver then nudged traces and vias apart wherever it could without touching the RAM wiring or any pair's length.

6

What is proven and what is not yet

  • Proven by checks on the copper: every net connected, 0.1 mm clearances, reference planes under fast lines, RAM routing identical to the verified solver output, all RAM timing rules, JLC assembly spacing, all 219 part rotations.
  • Simulated with generic models: RAM eyes and pair skew. Real numbers need the RK3566 and LPDDR4X IBIS buffer models, which Rockchip and the RAM vendor provide on request.
  • First field-solver result: openEMS puts our top-to-inner3 via at about 8.5 ps, below the 13.6 ps ngspice model. Length compensation tuned with the ngspice number may therefore over-correct the eight unequal-via pairs; routing them with equal vias avoids depending on either number. Next: proper de-embedded ports, the other via spans, and lossy eye diagrams with crosstalk.
  • Open: eight pairs with unequal P/N via counts (2.6 to 8.4 ps).
  • Final proof is the hardware: the Rockchip DDR loader prints read/write training margins at boot; run memtester and stressapptest at 1056 MHz, then 1560 MHz.
